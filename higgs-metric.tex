\begin{center}
\textbf{Metric on moduli of Higgs bundles} \\
Andrew Neitzke
\end{center}

{These are notes from a pair of lectures
given at the second Simons Workshop in Mathematics, July 15 and 17, 2024.
They describe a conjectural method for getting explicit information about
Hitchin's metrics on moduli spaces of Higgs bundles, based on the 
author's joint work with Gaiotto and Moore.
This method builds the metric out of data attached to the spectral curves: period coordinates together with integer Donaldson-Thomas-type invariants obeying wall-crossing formulas.}

\section{Introduction}

The aim of these lectures is to describe a \textit{conjectural} 
approach to more explicitly understanding the hyperk\"ahler metrics on 
moduli spaces of Higgs bundles. 
I will specialize to $\mathrm{SL}(2)$-Higgs bundles, but the
picture is similar for any reductive $G$.

Basic philosophy: replace $\mathrm{SL}(2)$-Higgs bundles and flat connections over a Riemann
surface $C$ by $\mathrm{GL}(1)$-Higgs bundles and flat connections over a branched double cover $\Sigma_{\phi_2}$
of $C$, called \textit{spectral curve}. The surprise is how well this 
works. Indeed, the conjecture says that the metric on $\mathcal M$
is constructed from two ingredients:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The \textit{periods} $Z_\gamma$ of the spectral curves $\Sigma_{\phi_2}$,
  \item A collection of integer ``Donaldson-Thomas invariants''
  $\operatorname{DT}(\gamma)$,
  which count (tropicalizations of) special Lagrangian discs
  in $T^*C$ with boundary on $\Sigma_{\phi_2}$.
\end{enumerate}

Although ultimately the metrics we want to understand
are smooth and have no obvious integrality properties,
this way of understanding them involves Donaldson-Thomas
invariants, cluster transformations, 
wall-crossing phenomena.
It is most digestible near asymptotic infinity, but in principle
gives information everywhere on $\mathcal M$.

The strategy of the lectures will be roughly:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The moduli space $\mathcal M$, its basic features and hyperk\"ahler structure; the weak form of the metric conjecture (now almost proven).
  \item The strong form of the conjecture (idea: calculate the nonabelian Hodge map), 
  and the available evidence that the conjecture is correct.
\end{enumerate}

This is a review of joint work with Davide Gaiotto and Greg Moore,
and follow-up work with Lotte Hollands and David Dumas; 
there is closely related 
work by Mazzeo-Swoboda-Weiss-Witt, Fredrickson, Mochizuki; 
the story is heavily inspired by Fock-Goncharov, Kontsevich-Soibelman, Hitchin,
Corlette, Donaldson, Simpson, Biquard-Boalch, and others.

\subsection{References}

The conjecture reviewed in these notes is mostly contained in 
the papers \cite{Gaiotto:2008cd,Gaiotto:2009hg},
which are joint work of mine with Davide Gaiotto and Greg
Moore.
In \cite{notes-hk} I reviewed some parts of the conjecture,
focusing on the abstract construction of hyperk\"ahler metrics from a
special K\"ahler base and Donaldson-Thomas invariants;
in these lectures I focus more on the examples provided by
moduli spaces of Higgs bundles,
and even more specifically, on one specific moduli space of
Higgs bundles with irregular singularity.

These works depend on many prior developments
in physics and mathematics. Here I can only single out a few
which were of singular importance (for more, see the references
in \cite{Gaiotto:2008cd,Gaiotto:2009hg}):
\begin{itemize}
\item The work \cite{Gaiotto:2008cd} 
originated in an attempt to understand the physical
meaning of the remarkable wall-crossing formula for generalized
Donaldson-Thomas invariants, given by Kontsevich-Soibelman \cite{ks1}.
\item Many of the key constructions in 
\cite{Gaiotto:2008cd} can
be understood as infinite-dimensional analogues 
of constructions used by Cecotti-Vafa and Dubrovin in 
$tt^*$ geometry \cite{Cecotti:1993rm,MR1213301}, with
additional inspiration from work of Bridgeland and 
Toledano Laredo \cite{stab-stokes}.
\item The application to Hitchin systems
in \cite{Gaiotto:2009hg} depended importantly on
the work of Fock-Goncharov on moduli spaces
of local systems over surfaces \cite{MR2233852},
as well as the foundational work
of Hitchin \cite{MR89a:32021} and Corlette, Donaldson, Simpson
\cite{MR965220,MR887285,MR944577} on Higgs bundles without
singularities, Simpson's extension to 
Higgs bundles with regular 
singularities \cite{hbnc}, and Biquard-Boalch for Higgs
bundles with wild ramification \cite{wnh}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background on Hitchin system}

\subsection{The unpunctured case}

Let me first recall the simplest case to define (though not the simplest
case to study!) The most fundamental reference is \cite{MR89a:32021}.
A very useful review can be found in
\cite{Wentworth2014} and references therein.

We fix a compact Riemann surface $C$ of genus $g_C \ge 2$.

\begin{defn}[$\mathrm{SL}(2)$-Higgs bundles] \label{def:higgs}
A $\mathrm{SL}(2)$-\textit{Higgs bundle} is a pair $(E, \varphi)$, where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $E$ is a holomorphic vector bundle of rank $2$ over $C$, with $\det E = \mathcal O$,
  \item $\varphi$ is a holomorphic section of $\operatorname{End} E \otimes K_C(P)$, with $\operatorname{Tr} \varphi = 0$.
\end{itemize}
\end{defn}
With respect to a local trivialization of $E$, then, $\varphi$ is represented
by a traceless $2 \times 2$ matrix whose entries are holomorphic $1$-forms.

\begin{defn}[Stability of $\mathrm{SL}(2)$-Higgs bundles]
We say $(E,\varphi)$ is \textit{stable} if, for all $\varphi$-invariant $E' \subset E$, we have $\deg E' < 0$.
We say $(E,\varphi)$ is \textit{strictly polystable} if it is a direct sum of two degree-zero $\varphi$-invariant line bundles.
We say $(E,\varphi)$ is \textit{polystable} if it is either stable or strictly polystable.
\end{defn}

\begin{prop}[Moduli space of Higgs bundles]
There is a moduli space $\mathcal M = \mathcal M(G,C)$ parameterizing
polystable $\mathrm{SL}(2)$-Higgs bundles $(E,\varphi)$ 
up to equivalence. 
$\mathcal M$ is a manifold away
from the locus of strictly-polystable Higgs bundles.
It has complex dimension $6 g_C - 6$.
It carries a natural complex structure $I_1$ and holomorphic symplectic form $\Omega_1$.
\end{prop}

The holomorphic symplectic form $\Omega_1$ comes from the fact 
that variations of the bundle $E$ are valued in $H^1(\operatorname{End} E)$,
while variations of the Higgs field $\varphi$ are
valued in $H^0(\operatorname{End} E \otimes K_C)$,
and the two are Serre dual.

\subsection{The Hitchin map}

Next we exhibit $\mathcal M$ as a complex integrable system,
i.e. a holomorphic Lagrangian fibration over a base 
$\mathcal B$.

Given a Higgs bundle $(E,\varphi) \in \mathcal M$ and $z \in C$,
we can consider the eigenvalues of $\varphi(z)$. As $z$ varies
these sweep out a curve $\Sigma$:
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma = \{ (z,\lambda): \lambda^2 + \phi_2(z) = 0\} \, \subset T^*C \, ,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \phi_2 = - \frac12 \operatorname{Tr} \varphi^2 \, .
\end{equation}
The projection $\rho: \Sigma \to C$ given by $\rho(z,\lambda) = z$ is a branched double cover,
ramified at the zeroes of $\phi_2$.

\begin{defn}[Hitchin base and Hitchin map]
Define the \textit{Hitchin base} $\mathcal B = H^0(C, K_C^2)$.
The \textit{Hitchin map} is the map $\pi: \mathcal M \to \mathcal B$ given by
\begin{equation}
  (E, \varphi) \mapsto \phi_2 = - \frac12 \operatorname{Tr} \varphi^2 \, .
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

\begin{prop}[Hitchin map has Lagrangian fibers] The fibers
$\mathcal M_{\phi_2} = \pi^{-1}(\vec\phi)$ 
are compact complex Lagrangian subsets of $(\mathcal M, I_1, \Omega_1)$. (In particular, $\dim_\mathbb C \mathcal B = \frac{1}{2} \dim_\mathbb C \mathcal M$.)
\end{prop}

We can say more precisely what the fibers are,
over most of the Hitchin base:

\begin{defn}[Singular locus and smooth locus]
The \textit{singular locus} $\mathcal B_\mathrm{sing} \subset \mathcal B$ is the set of $\vec\phi \in \mathcal B$
for which $\Sigma_{\vec \phi}$ is singular.
The \textit{smooth locus} is $\mathcal B_\mathrm{reg} = \mathcal B \setminus \mathcal B_\mathrm{sing}$.
It consists of those $\phi_2$ which have only simple zeroes ($4 g_C - 4$ of them).
Also let $\mathcal M_\mathrm{reg} = \pi^{-1}(\mathcal B_\mathrm{reg})$.
\end{defn}

\begin{prop}[Fibers of the Hitchin map over $\mathcal B_\mathrm{reg}$]
Suppose $\vec\phi \in \mathcal B_\mathrm{reg}$.
Then $\mathcal M_{\phi_2}$ is a torsor over the compact complex torus $\operatorname{Prym} (\rho: \Sigma_{\phi_2} \to C)$. 
After choosing spin structures on 
$C$ and $\Sigma_{\phi_2}$, we can identify $\mathcal M_{\phi_2}$ with the space of flat $\mathrm{U}(1)$-connections $\nabla$ over $\Sigma_{\phi_2}$, equipped with a trivialization of $\det \rho_* \nabla$.
\end{prop}

So we reach the following picture: a point $\vec\phi \in \mathcal B_\mathrm{reg}$
gives a smooth spectral curve $\Sigma_{\phi_2}$; the torus
$\mathcal M_{\phi_2}$ is a space of flat $\mathrm{U}(1)$-connections
over $\Sigma_{\phi_2}$.
When $\vec\phi \in \mathcal B_\mathrm{sing}$, $\mathcal M_{\phi_2}$ is compact,
but generally singular.

\subsection{The hyperk\"ahler metric}

A key fact about $\mathcal M$ is that it carries a canonically
defined hyperk\"ahler metric $g$. However, $g$ is not easily
written in closed form.
To construct $g$, one needs to consider \textit{Hitchin's equation}:
given a Higgs bundle $(E,\varphi)$ this is a PDE
for a Hermitian metric $h$ in $E$, written
\begin{equation} \label{eq:hitchin-equation}
  F_{D_h} + [\varphi, \varphi^{\dagger_h}] = 0.
\end{equation}
Here $D_h$ denotes the Chern connection in $(E,h)$,
the unique $h$-unitary connection compatible with
the holomorphic structure of $E$.

Now there is the following key theorem:
\begin{thm}[Existence of harmonic metrics] \label{thm:hitchin-existence}
The equation \eqref{eq:hitchin-equation} has a solution $h$ for each $(E,\varphi)$;
this $h$ is unique up to scalar multiple.
We call $h$ the \textit{harmonic metric}.
\end{thm}

Using \autoref{thm:hitchin-existence}
one can define Hitchin's metric on $\mathcal M$, as follows.
Given a tangent vector $v$ to $\mathcal M$ whose norm we wish to calculate, we represent $v$ by a family of Higgs bundles
$(E_t,\varphi_t)$, with harmonic metrics $h_t$.
Identifying the underlying Hermitian bundles with a single $(E,h)$
we have an arc of unitary connections $D_t$ and skew-Hermitian 
Higgs fields $\Phi_t = \varphi_t - \varphi_t^\dagger$ 
on $(E,h)$, determined up to gauge transformations i.e. 
automorphisms of $(E,h)$.
In particular, differentiating at $t = 0$ gives a pair
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} t}\Bigg\rvert_{t=0} (D_t, \Phi_t) = 
(\dot A, \dot \Phi) \in \Omega^1(\mathfrak{su}(E)) \oplus \Omega^{1}(\mathfrak{su}(E)),
\end{equation}
defined up to gauge transformations.
Then the norm of $v$ is the $L^2$ norm
\begin{equation} \label{eq:l2-metric-def}
  g(v,v) = \int_C \lVert \dot A \rVert^2 + \lVert \dot \Phi \rVert^2
\end{equation}
where for $(\dot A, \dot \Phi)$ we choose the representative
\textit{minimizing} the norm.

\begin{remark}[Hyperk\"ahler quotient] I have not really explained
why the metric $g$ constructed in this way turns out to 
be hyperk\"ahler, or even K\"ahler. The most conceptual explanation
of this comes by viewing the construction in terms of 
an infinite-dimensional hyperk\"ahler quotient.
This was explained by
Hitchin in \cite{MR89a:32021}.

\end{remark}

\subsection{The semiflat metric}

Now we want to describe a first approximation to Hitchin's metric.

The regular part $\mathcal B_\mathrm{reg}$ of the Hitchin base 
carries a (rigid) special K\"ahler structure 
in the sense of \cite{Freed:1997dp}, as follows.

The deck transformation $\sigma: \Sigma_{\phi_2} \to \Sigma_{\phi_2}$
induces an action on $H_1(\Sigma, \mathbb Z)$.
Let $H_1(\Sigma_{\phi_2}, \mathbb Z)^\pm$ denote the $\pm 1$-eigenspaces.

\begin{defn}[Charge lattice]
The \textit{charge lattice} is
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_{\phi_2} = H_1(\Sigma_{\phi_2}, \mathbb Z)^- \, .
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

Let $\langle , \rangle$ denote the intersection pairing on $\Gamma$
and $\langle\!\langle , \rangle\!\rangle$ its inverse on $\Gamma^*_\mathbb R$.
These lattices make up a local system $\Gamma$ over $\mathcal B_\mathrm{reg}$.
We write local formulas using a local
trivialization of $\Gamma_\mathbb R$ by ``$A$ and $B$ cycles''
obeying
\begin{equation}
  \langle A^I, A^J \rangle=0, \quad \langle B_I, B_J \rangle=0, \quad \langle A^I,B_J \rangle = \delta^I_J.
\end{equation}

\begin{defn}[Period map]
Let $\lambda$ denote the tautological (Liouville) holomorphic $1$-form
on $\operatorname{Tot}[K_C]$.
The \textit{period map} is the map
\begin{equation}
   Z: \Gamma_{\phi_2} \to \mathbb C, \qquad Z_\gamma = \oint_\gamma \lambda
\end{equation}
which we could also view as an element $Z \in \Gamma^*_\mathbb C$.
\end{defn}
Then we have $\mathrm{d} Z \in \Omega^1(\mathcal M) \otimes \Gamma_\mathbb C^*$,
and we can define a $2$-form $\langle\!\langle \mathrm{d} Z, \mathrm{d} \overline{Z} \rangle\!\rangle$,
given concretely by
\begin{equation}
  \langle\!\langle \mathrm{d} Z, \mathrm{d} \overline{Z} \rangle\!\rangle = \sum_{I = 1}^{r} \mathrm{d} Z_{A^I} \wedge \mathrm{d} \overline{Z}_{B_I} - \mathrm{d} Z_{B_I} \wedge \mathrm{d} \overline{Z}_{A^I} \, .
\end{equation}

\begin{prop}[Positivity] 
$\langle\!\langle \mathrm{d} Z, \mathrm{d} \overline{Z} \rangle\!\rangle$ is a positive $(1,1)$-form on $\mathcal B_\mathrm{reg}$. 
\end{prop}
Thus it defines
a K\"ahler metric on $\mathcal B_\mathrm{reg}$.

Now we consider the fiber directions.
As we have said, $\mathcal M_{\phi_2}$ is a space of flat $U(1)$-connections
over $\Sigma_{\phi_2}$. 
In particular, for each $\gamma \in \Gamma_{\phi_2}$ 
there is a corresponding
holonomy $\theta_\gamma: \mathcal M_{\phi_2} \to \mathbb R / 2 \pi \mathbb Z$.
Their differentials can be assembled into
  $\mathrm{d} \theta \in \Omega^1(\mathcal M) \otimes \Gamma^*_\mathbb R$.
If we choose a basis for $\Gamma_{\phi_2}$ 
then we get $\mathbb R / 2 \pi \mathbb Z$-valued coordinates $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_{2r}$
on $\mathcal M_{\phi_2}$.

\begin{defn}[Semiflat metric]
The \textit{semiflat metric}  $g^{\mathrm{sf}}$ on $\mathcal M_\mathrm{reg}$ is the metric 
whose K\"ahler form in structure $I_1$ is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:omega1-sf}
  \omega^{\mathrm{sf}}_1 = 2 \langle\!\langle \mathrm{d} Z, \mathrm{d} \overline{Z} \rangle\!\rangle - \langle\!\langle \mathrm{d} \theta, \mathrm{d} \theta \rangle\!\rangle.
\end{equation}
\end{defn}                                   

Now we can formulate the weak version of our conjecture.
It says that, away from the singular fibers, Hitchin's
metric is exponentially close to the semiflat metric.

\begin{defn}[Threshold]
Fix $\phi_2 \in \mathcal B_\mathrm{reg}$. Then $\lvert \phi_2 \rvert$ is a singular flat metric on $C$, with singularities at the
zeroes of $\phi_2$. The \textit{threshold} $M(\phi_2)$
is twice the length of the shortest inextendible geodesic
(saddle connection) in this metric.\footnote{Here we redefined the threshold by a factor of 2 relative to some references.}
\end{defn}

\begin{conj}[Weak metric conjecture] \label{conj:weak-metric}
Fix $\phi_2 \in \mathcal B_\mathrm{reg}$.
As we rescale $\phi_2 \to R^2 \phi_2$ with $R > 0$,
Hitchin's metric approaches the semiflat metric
exponentially fast:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:semiflat-conj}
  g = g^{\mathrm{sf}} + O(\mathrm e^{-2 R M(\phi_2)}) \, .
\end{equation}
\end{conj}

The meaning of the conjecture is that the naive cartoon drawing of the
torus fibration is exponentially close to being the correct metric picture.
It is an instance of the Strominger-Yau-Zaslow picture of Calabi-Yau
manifolds.

The sharpest theorem to date relating to \autoref{conj:weak-metric} is
given in \cite{mochizuki2024comparison}. On
the section $\theta_\gamma = 0$
it proves \eqref{eq:semiflat-conj} on the nose; 
on the full moduli space, it \textit{almost} proves
\autoref{conj:weak-metric}, but has the 
exponent $RM$ instead of the desired $2RM$.



\section{Our strategy}

Now let us explain our strategy for describing the hyperk\"ahler metric on $\mathcal M$.

\subsection{The other complex structures of \texorpdfstring{$\mathcal M$}{M}}

So far we have focused on just one of the complex
structures of $\mathcal M$, which we called $I_1$,
and its holomorphic symplectic form $\Omega_1$.
We have also said that $\mathcal M$ is hyperk\"ahler, which means it has more structure.
Our construction of the metric needs to use all that structure.

The definition of hyperk\"ahler manifold says that 
$\mathcal M$ has complex structures $I_1, I_2, I_3$ obeying
$I_1 I_2 = -I_2 I_1 = I_3$ and cyclic permutations,
with corresponding K\"ahler forms $\omega_1, \omega_2, \omega_3$.
The holomorphic symplectic forms are related to the
K\"ahler forms by $\Omega_1 = \omega_2 + \mathrm i \omega_3$ 
and cyclic permutations.
In fact $\mathcal M$ has a whole family of complex structures 
$I^\zeta$, $\zeta \in \mathbb C\mathbb P^1$ (where
$I^{\zeta=0} = I_1, I^{\zeta = \mathrm i} = I_2, I^{\zeta = 1} = I_3$),
and corresponding holomorphic symplectic forms $\Omega^\zeta$.
What are they?

Given a $\mathrm{SL}(2)$-Higgs bundle $(E,\varphi)$ and 
solution $h$ of Hitchin's equations \eqref{eq:hitchin-equation}
there is a corresponding $1$-parameter family of flat
$\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)$-connections over $C$:
\begin{equation}
  \nabla(\zeta) = \zeta^{-1} \varphi + D + \zeta \varphi^\dagger.
\end{equation}
Let $\mathcal M^\flat$
be the moduli space of flat reductive 
$\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)$-connections over $C$. 
$\mathcal M^\flat$ carries a complex structure $I^\flat$
and a holomorphic symplectic
structure $\Omega^{ABG}$ (Atiyah-Bott-Goldman).

\begin{prop} \label{prop:generic-structures-flat-connections} For any $\zeta \in \mathbb C^\times$,
the map $(E,\varphi) \mapsto \nabla(\zeta)$ identifies
\begin{equation}
  (\mathcal M, I^\zeta, \Omega^\zeta) \xrightarrow\sim (\mathcal M^\flat, I^\flat, \Omega^{ABG}) \, .
\end{equation}
\end{prop}

So the complex structures $I^\zeta$, $\zeta \in \mathbb C^\times$, look very different
from $I^0$ and $I^\infty$.

\subsection{Local Darboux coordinates}

\autoref{prop:generic-structures-flat-connections} 
implies that any holomorphic function $\mathcal X$ on $\mathcal M^\flat$,
when applied to
the flat connection $\nabla(\zeta)$, becomes a holomorphic
function on $(\mathcal M, I^\zeta)$.
Extending this to coordinate systems, any holomorphic 
Darboux coordinate system $\{\mathcal X_i\}$ on $(\mathcal M^\flat, \Omega^{ABG})$
becomes a holomorphic Darboux coordinate system on
$(\mathcal M, I^\zeta)$.

Our aim is to calculate holomorphic Darboux
coordinates $\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta)$ of a given fixed Higgs bundle.
Since $\nabla(\zeta)$ varies
holomorphically with $\zeta$, the coordinates
$\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta)$ do as well; so they can be thought of
as functions on the \textit{twistor space} of $\mathcal M$.

\begin{itemize}
\item {\bf Q}: Which holomorphic Darboux coordinate system on
$(\mathcal M^\flat, \Omega^{ABG})$ will we use? 
{\bf A}: We actually
will not use just one: instead, as we move around on the
Hitchin base $\mathcal B$ and/or vary the argument of $\zeta$, 
we will choose different coordinate
systems in different regions, separated by codimension-1 ``walls.''

\end{minipage}
\medskip

\item {\bf Q}: Why do you need to have such a complicated structure? {\bf A}: Because we want to study
these coordinates through their analytic properties in the
$\zeta$-plane, and only certain coordinates will be \textit{good} in the sense of having 
simple analytic behavior
as $\zeta \to 0, \infty$; moreover, which coordinates are good changes as
we move around on $\mathcal B$ or vary the argument of $\zeta$.

\item {\bf Q}: How does this help you get the metric? {\bf A}: On $(\mathcal M, I_1)$ we 
already have the holomorphic
symplectic form $\Omega_1 = \omega_2 + \mathrm i \omega_3$. All that
is missing is the third symplectic form $\omega_1$.
Once we have holomorphic Darboux coordinate functions $\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta)$,
we can specialize them to say $\zeta = 1$ and get a formula
for the holomorphic symplectic form $\Omega^{\zeta = 1} = \Omega_3 = \omega_1 + \mathrm i \omega_2$; then the desired $\omega_1$ is just
$\operatorname{Re} \Omega^{\zeta = 1}$.

\item {\bf Q}: Won't the jumping of the $\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta)$ at the walls
cause a problem? {\bf A}: No, the jumps are always by \textit{symplectomorphisms},
so that even though $\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta)$ jumps, $\Omega^\zeta$ doesn't.
\end{itemize}

\section{An irregular extension} \label{sec:irregular-extension}

To go further, we introduce some of the simplest model examples,
where we can actually describe the $\mathcal X_\gamma$ concretely. These examples
however are slightly outside our original setup: we need to allow
the Higgs bundles to have singularities. To get the very simplest
setup we will actually allow \textit{irregular} singularities.


\subsection{Definitions}

We let $C = \mathbb C\mathbb P^1$, with the usual inhomogeneous coordinate $z$, 
and use objects which are \textit{meromorphic},
with poles at $z = \infty$, rather than holomorphic.

Fix a polynomial $q$ of degree $N \in 2 \mathbb N + 1$.
(In fact we only need to know $q$ modulo polynomials of degree
$\le \frac{N-3}{2}$.)

\begin{defn}[Irregular Higgs bundles of type $q$]
An \textit{irregular $\mathrm{SL}(2)$-Higgs bundle} of type $q$ is
a pair $(E,\varphi)$, where:
\begin{itemize} 
  \item $E$ is a 
meromorphic vector bundle, holomorphic away from 
$z = \infty$, 
with a valuation $\nu_\infty$ on meromorphic sections,
and a nowhere-vanishing section $\eta \in \det E$ with $\nu_\infty(\eta) = 0$,
 \item $\varphi$ is a traceless meromorphic section of $\operatorname{End}(E) \otimes K_C$,
 holomorphic away from $z = \infty$, obeying 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:phi2-polynomial}
  \phi_2 = -\frac12 \operatorname{Tr} \varphi^2 = (q(z) + l(z)) \, \mathrm{d} z^2
\end{equation}
with $\deg l < \frac{N}{2} - 1$,
\end{itemize}
such that for any meromorphic section $s$ of $E$, 
\begin{equation}
\nu_\infty(z^{m} s) = \nu_\infty(s) + m, \qquad \nu_\infty(\varphi \cdot s) = \nu_\infty(s) + \frac{N}{2} \, .  
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

Everything we said about ordinary $\mathrm{SL}(2)$-Higgs bundles
has an analogue for these irregular ones, as explained in \cite{wnh} (plus epsilon).
Here I write it in slightly different language.
In particular:

\begin{defn}[Adapted metric]
Suppose $(E,\varphi)$ is an irregular Higgs bundle. An \textit{adapted metric} in $(E,\varphi)$ is a Hermitian metric $h$ in $E$, such that
\begin{equation}
\log h(s,s) \sim 2 \nu_\infty(s) \log \lvert z \rvert \quad \text{ as } \lvert z \rvert \to \infty \, . 
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

\begin{thm}[Existence of adapted metrics for irregular Higgs bundles]
If $(E,\varphi)$ is an irregular Higgs bundle, there is an adapted metric $h$ in $E$ which obeys Hitchin's equations \eqref{eq:hitchin-equation}, and $h$ is unique up to scalar multiple.
\end{thm}

\begin{thm}[Moduli space of irregular Higgs bundles of type $q$]\footnote{As far as I understand, not all 
details have fully appeared in print. I write ``Theorem'' here, a little optimistically.}
There is a moduli space $\mathcal M(q)$ parameterizing irregular Higgs bundles of type $q$ up to equivalence.
$\mathcal M(q)$ is a smooth manifold, of dimension $N-1$.
The formula \eqref{eq:l2-metric-def} defines a complete hyperk\"ahler metric on $\mathcal M(q)$.
\end{thm}

So this is a continuous family of hyperk\"ahler spaces, parameterized by the choice of $q$.
The space of polynomials $q$ has dimension $N+1$, but we should mod out by automorphisms
of $\mathbb C\mathbb P^1$ preserving $z = \infty$ and by low-degree shifts,
so the effective number of parameters in the family 
is $(N+1) - 2 - (\frac{N-3}{2} + 1) = \frac{N-1}{2}$.

The Hitchin base $\mathcal B(q)$ in this case is just the space of polynomials 
$P_2 = q + l$ appearing above, with $q$ fixed and $l$ varying.
It has complex dimension $\frac12(N-1)$ as it should.

\begin{example}
The case $N=3$ is the first interesting one. Here we pick
\begin{equation}
  q(z) = z^3 + \Lambda z
\end{equation}
for $\Lambda \in \mathbb C$. Then $\mathcal M(q)$ is a hyperk\"ahler space of complex dimension $2$,
depending on the choice of $\Lambda$. The Hitchin base is just $\mathcal B(q) = \mathbb C$.
If $\Lambda = 0$ there is a single singular fiber, which is a cuspidal torus.
If $\Lambda \neq 0$ there are two, which are both nodal tori.
\end{example}


\section{The coordinates} \label{sec:coordinates}

Now we describe the holomorphic coordinates $\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta)$ on $(\mathcal M, I^\zeta)$
which we will use.
We consider only the case of irregular Higgs bundles
on $\mathbb C\mathbb P^1$ as we have been discussing above.
(A very similar construction works in the case of
Higgs bundles with regular singularities.)

\subsection{Defining the coordinates}

Fix $\phi_2 \in \mathcal B$ and $\zeta \in \mathbb C^\times$.

\begin{defn}[$\zeta$-trajectories of a quadratic differential]
A \textit{$\zeta$-trajectory} of $\phi_2$ is a path on $C$
along which
$\zeta^{-1} \sqrt{-\phi_2}$ (with either choice of sign
for $\sqrt{-\phi_2}$) is a
\textit{real} and nowhere vanishing form.
\end{defn}

\begin{prop}[$\zeta$-trajectories give a foliation] The
$\zeta$-trajectories are the leaves of a singular
foliation of $C$, with singularities at the zeroes
and poles of $\phi_2$. 
At each zero of $\phi_2$, the foliation
by $\zeta$-trajectories has a three-pronged singularity,
as shown below.

$N+2$ rays $r_i$ at infinity, such that any generic $\zeta$-trajectory is asymptotic

Fix an interior edge $E \in T(\phi_2,\zeta)$.
$E$ determines a class $\gamma \in \Gamma_{\phi_2}$, shown

To define $\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta)$, we consider the connection
$\nabla(\zeta)$ restricted to the quadrilateral shown.
Its space of flat sections is a $2$-dimensional vector space $V$,
equipped with $4$ distinguished lines $\ell_i \subset V$:
$\ell_i$ consists of the flat sections
which have exponentially \textit{decaying} norm 
as we go to infinity along a leaf of $T(\phi_2,\zeta)$ in the $i$-th direction.
Said otherwise, the $\ell_i$ give $4$ points of $\mathbb C\mathbb P^1$.
We define $\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta)$ to be the
$\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)$-invariant cross-ratio of these $4$ points:
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta) = - \frac{(\ell_1 \wedge \ell_2)(\ell_3 \wedge \ell_4)}{(\ell_2 \wedge \ell_3) (\ell_4 \wedge \ell_1)} .
\end{equation}
This definition appears in
Fock-Goncharov \cite{MR2233852}; it is a complexification of the notion of shear coordinate.
\end{defn}

Applying \autoref{def:fg-coordinate} for all edges $E$
of $T(\phi_2,\vartheta)$ gives functions
$\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta)$ with $\gamma$ running over a basis for $\Gamma$. 
We extend to arbitrary $\gamma$ by requiring
$\mathcal X_\gamma \mathcal X_\mu = \mathcal X_{\gamma+\mu}$.
These are local
Darboux coordinates:
\begin{equation}
  \Omega^\zeta = \langle\!\langle \mathrm{d} \log \mathcal X(\zeta), \mathrm{d} \log \mathcal X(\zeta) \rangle\!\rangle.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Asymptotic behavior of the coordinates}

The main asymptotic property of the coordinates $\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta)$ is:
\begin{conj}[Asymptotic behavior of Darboux coordinates] \label{conj:asymptotic-darboux}
Fix a point of $\mathcal M$.
Then, as $\zeta \to 0$ along any ray,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta) \sim \exp \left(\zeta^{-1} Z_\gamma + \mathrm i \theta_\gamma + c_\gamma \right)
\end{equation}
where the constants $c_\gamma = \int_\gamma \alpha$
for some $\alpha \in \Omega^{1,1}(\Sigma)$.
Moreover, if all $\theta_\gamma = 0$, then
all $c_\gamma = 0$, so in that case
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta) \sim \exp \left(\zeta^{-1} Z_\gamma\right).
\end{equation}
\end{conj}
(The idea: it would follow from the exact WKB method applied to the connections $\nabla(\zeta) = \zeta^{-1} \varphi + \cdots$.)

\subsection{Piecewise analytic behavior of the coordinates}

As we vary $(\phi_2, \zeta)$, the
function $\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta)$ is only piecewise 
smooth: it suffers
a jump whenever the triangulation $T(\phi_2, \zeta)$
changes. 
The simplest kind of jump is shown below:

\medskip
\noindent
\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}

\makebox[\linewidth]{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio=true,scale=1.25]{figures/higgs-metric-11-crop.pdf}}

\end{minipage}
\medskip

This jump is associated with the ``saddle connection'' 
connecting two zeroes of $\phi_2$, 
appearing in the middle of the figure.
Such a saddle connection can only 
appear when $\zeta^{-1} Z_\mu \in \mathbb R_-$.
The coordinates on the two sides of the jump are related by:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal X_\gamma \to \mathcal X_\gamma(1 + \mathcal X_\mu)^{\langle \mu,\gamma \rangle}.
\end{equation}
A similar (but more intricate) 
phenomenon occurs when we cross a $(\phi_2, \zeta)$
for which an annulus of closed trajectories appears: 
then the $\mathcal X_\gamma$ undergo a jump of the form
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal X_\gamma \to \mathcal X_\gamma(1 - \mathcal X_\mu)^{-2 \langle \mu,\gamma \rangle}.
\end{equation}
Both of these are instances of the following general
structure:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ks-map}
  \mathcal X_\gamma \to \mathcal X_\gamma(1 - \sigma(\mu) \mathcal X_\mu)^{\operatorname{DT}(\mu) \langle \mu,\gamma \rangle}
\end{equation}
where for a saddle connection
we have $\operatorname{DT}(\mu) = +1$ and $\sigma(\mu) = -1$, while
for a closed loop we have $\operatorname{DT}(\mu) = -2$ and $\sigma(\mu) = +1$.

Fix $\phi_2$ and just let $\zeta$
vary. Then $\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta)$ depends on 
$\zeta$ in a piecewise-analytic way: the collection
$\{\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta)\}_{\gamma \in \Gamma}$ jumps 
at various rays $\ell$ in the $\zeta$-plane. 

\medskip
\noindent
\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}

\makebox[\linewidth]{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio=true,scale=0.85]{figures/higgs-metric-14-crop.pdf}}

\end{minipage}
\medskip

At each such ray, the jump is a
product of transformations of the form \eqref{eq:ks-map},
where the $\mu$ in \eqref{eq:ks-map} can be any
$\mu \in \Gamma$ such that $Z_\mu / \zeta \in \mathbb R_-$
along $\ell$.

\subsection{The integral equation} \label{sec:integral-equations}

We are building up an elaborate structure,
but it is only going to be useful if it allows
us to say something concrete about
$\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta)$. Here is one approach:

\begin{conj}[Integral equation for $\theta_\gamma = 0$] \label{conj:integral-equation}
When all $\theta_\gamma = 0$,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:integral-equation}
  \mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta) = \mathcal X_\gamma^{\mathrm{sf}}(\zeta) \exp \left[ \frac{1}{4\pi \mathrm i} \sum_{\mu \in \Gamma} \operatorname{DT}(\mu) \langle \gamma,\mu \rangle \int_{Z_\mu \mathbb R_-} \frac{\mathrm{d} \zeta'}{\zeta'} \frac{\zeta' + \zeta}{\zeta'-\zeta} \log(1 - \sigma(\mu) \mathcal X_\mu(\zeta')) \right]
\end{equation}
where 
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal X_\gamma^{\mathrm{sf}}(\zeta) = \exp \left( \zeta^{-1} Z_\gamma + \zeta \overline{Z}_\gamma \right).
\end{equation}
The signs $\sigma(\mu) = \pm 1$ obey the relation
\begin{equation}
  \sigma(\mu) \sigma(\mu') = (-1)^{\langle \mu,\mu' \rangle} \sigma(\mu + \mu').
\end{equation}
\end{conj}

The functions $\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta)$ appear on both sides of
\eqref{eq:integral-equation}. Thus \eqref{eq:integral-equation} is an integral equation, which needs to be solved for 
the whole collection $\{\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta)\}_{\gamma \in \Gamma}$
at once, rather than an integral formula.

\begin{itemize}
  \item {\bf Q}: Where does this equation come from? {\bf A}: It would lead to $\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta)$ with
  the right analytic properties in the $\zeta$-plane: asymptotics as $\zeta \to 0,\infty$
  and jumps at the rays $Z_\mu / \zeta \in \mathbb R_-$ with $\operatorname{DT}(\mu) \neq 0$.
  The optimistic hope is that these analytic properties are strong enough
  to \textit{determine} $\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta)$.

  \item {\bf Q}: How do you actually solve it? {\bf A}: By iteration: pick
  $\mathcal X_\gamma(\zeta) = \mathcal X_\gamma^{\mathrm{sf}}(\zeta)$ as initial guess, 
  and then iterate.

  \item {\bf Q}: Why would you think that that iteration would converge?
  {\bf A1}: If all $\lvert Z_\gamma \rvert$ are large enough, and $\operatorname{DT}(\mu)$ doesn't grow too fast
  as a function of $\mu$ (e.g. if only finitely many are nonzero)
  saddle-point estimates show 
  the iteration defines a contraction mapping, so it must
  converge to a (unique) fixed point.
  {\bf A2}: Actually, experimentally it seems that
  it converges even when the $\lvert Z_\gamma \rvert$ are small! This
  deserves an explanation.

  \item {\bf Q}: How does this lead to the weak
  conjecture, \autoref{conj:weak-metric}, from the previous lecture? {\bf A}: 
  If we substitute $\mathcal X = \mathcal X^{\mathrm{sf}}$, the log in the
  integrand is bounded above by $\mathrm e^{-2 \lvert Z_\mu \rvert}$;
  thus we expect that the first step of the iteration is already
  suppressed by $\mathrm e^{-2 M}$ where $M$ is the minimum $\lvert Z_\mu \rvert$
  for which $\operatorname{DT}(\mu) \neq 0$, and later steps
  should be further exponentially suppressed. That suggests
  that just truncating to the zeroth iteration (i.e. taking $g^{\mathrm{sf}}$)
  would already give a result exponentially close to the true metric,
  and the accuracy will improve with each iteration we take.
  In particular we can truncate to the \textit{first} iteration.
  Working this out leads to
  \begin{equation}
    g = g^{\mathrm{sf}} - \frac{2}{\pi} \sum_{\mu \in \Gamma} \operatorname{DT}(\mu) K_0\left( 2 \lvert Z_\mu \rvert \right) \mathrm{d} \lvert Z_\mu \rvert^2 + \cdots
  \end{equation}
  where $K_0$ is the modified Bessel function.
  Note that $K_0(x) \sim \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2x}} \mathrm e^{-x}$,
  so $g - g^{\mathrm{sf}}$ is exponentially suppressed as $\mathrm e^{-2M}$.
  The omitted terms $\cdots$ should be of order $\mathrm e^{-4M}$.

  \item {\bf Q}: What about the rest of the moduli space, where $\theta_\gamma \neq 0$?
  {\bf A}: Here everything we can say is more tentative. 
  There is an extension of the integral equation to include $\theta_\gamma$, but the
  constants $c_\gamma$ appearing in \autoref{conj:asymptotic-darboux} create a complication. 
  I tentatively believe that this complication should not affect the weak conjecture 
  (because the $c_\gamma$ should decay like ${\mathrm e}^{-2 R M}$, so they only affect the
  metric at that order),
  but it does affect the strong conjecture. Worse, there is numerical evidence that
  when $\theta_\gamma \neq 0$, the convergence of the integral iteration can fail for 
  small $\lvert Z_\gamma \rvert$; so the conjecture needs to be modified if it is to make
  sense everywhere on the moduli space.
\end{itemize}

\section{Numerical tests}

The strongest evidence supporting \autoref{conj:integral-equation} is numerical,
given in \cite{dumas2020opers}.

\subsection{The Hitchin section}

\begin{defn}[Hitchin section]
Let $\mathcal O(\alpha)$ denote the trivial meromorphic bundle over $\mathbb C \mathbb P^1$, 
with a valuation $\nu_\infty$ given by the usual pole order at $z = \infty$ 
shifted by $-\alpha$.
Then given $\phi_2 = P_2(z) \mathrm{d} z^2 \in \mathcal B(q)$ we consider the Higgs bundle
$(E,\varphi)$:
\begin{equation}
  E = \mathcal O\left(\frac{N}{4}\right) \oplus \mathcal O\left(-\frac{N}{4}\right), \qquad \varphi = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -P_2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
This gives a section of the Hitchin map for $\mathcal M(q)$.
\end{defn}

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{$\mathcal X_\gamma$}{Xgamma} in an example}

$2$ out of the $5$ possible cross-ratios.

We take the concrete example
\begin{equation}
